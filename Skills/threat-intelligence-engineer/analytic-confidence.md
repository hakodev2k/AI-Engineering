# Analytic Confidence and Estimative Language

## Purpose
Communicate uncertainty consistently so consumers understand likelihood, evidence strength, and limitations.

## When to use
Use in every intelligence assessment containing inference, forecast, attribution, or incomplete evidence.

## Inputs
Evidence quality, source reliability, corroboration, alternatives, information gaps, organizational probability vocabulary.

## Context to inspect
Review local estimative-language standards and how the consumer interprets probability and confidence.

## Core knowledge
Likelihood answers how probable a proposition is; confidence answers how strongly evidence supports the judgment. Avoid mixing them or using ambiguous terms.

## Procedure
1. Identify each material analytic judgment.
2. Determine whether it expresses fact, likelihood, or forecast.
3. Assess evidence quality, independence, consistency, and gaps.
4. Choose probability language from the approved scale.
5. Assign confidence separately.
6. Explain major confidence drivers and limitations.
7. Remove unsupported numeric precision.
8. Check language for consistency across the product.

## Decision points
Use numeric ranges only when consumers understand them and evidence supports calibration; otherwise use standardized verbal terms.

## Common failure patterns
High confidence interpreted as high probability, vague words such as “likely” without a scale, confidence inflation, and burying caveats.

## Verification
Independent readers interpret likelihood and confidence consistently and can identify why confidence is limited.

## Expected output
Assessment language with explicit likelihood, confidence, and concise uncertainty rationale.

## Stop conditions
Stop and clarify terminology when organizational scales conflict or the requested certainty exceeds available evidence.
