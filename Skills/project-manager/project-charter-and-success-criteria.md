# Project Charter and Success Criteria

## Purpose
Establish an actionable project mandate that aligns outcomes, scope, authority, constraints, stakeholders, and measurable success before execution accelerates.

## When to use
Use when initiating a project, materially re-baselining it, or taking over an ambiguous initiative. Do not use a charter to replace detailed delivery plans or product requirements.

## Inputs
Business case, sponsor goals, expected outcomes, known scope, deadlines, budget constraints, stakeholder list, dependencies, and governance expectations.

## Preconditions
A sponsor or accountable decision-maker is identifiable and the initiative has enough definition to distinguish desired outcomes from implementation assumptions.

## Context to inspect
Review related programs, existing commitments, organizational priorities, prior decisions, regulatory constraints, resource commitments, and conflicting definitions of success.

## Core knowledge
A charter creates decision boundaries. Senior project management distinguishes outputs from outcomes, assumptions from facts, and hard constraints from negotiable targets. Success criteria should be measurable and include delivery, business, quality, and operational dimensions where relevant.

## Procedure
1. Identify the sponsor, accountable owner, and primary beneficiaries.
2. State the business problem and intended outcomes without prescribing unnecessary implementation.
3. Define in-scope and explicitly out-of-scope areas.
4. Capture hard constraints, assumptions, dependencies, and known risks.
5. Define measurable success criteria and acceptance evidence.
6. Establish decision rights, escalation paths, and governance cadence.
7. Identify major milestones and target dates at a level appropriate for initiation.
8. Validate resource and budget assumptions with accountable owners.
9. Resolve contradictory stakeholder expectations before baselining.
10. Obtain explicit sponsor agreement and record unresolved conditions.

## Decision points
Use a lightweight charter for small, low-risk initiatives; use stronger governance for cross-functional, regulated, high-cost, or irreversible work. If outcome ownership is unclear, resolve accountability before detailed scheduling.

## Common failure patterns
Treating a deadline as the project purpose, hiding exclusions, vague success measures, undocumented assumptions, assigning responsibility without authority, and obtaining ceremonial approval while conflicts remain.

## Verification
Confirm the sponsor can explain why the project exists, scope boundaries are testable, success measures have owners and evidence sources, and governance/escalation paths are understood.

## Expected output
An approved project charter with outcomes, scope, constraints, success criteria, governance, ownership, assumptions, and major milestones.

## Stop conditions
Escalate when no accountable sponsor exists, fundamental objectives conflict, mandatory constraints make success infeasible, or authorization to proceed is absent.
