# Technical Discovery

## Purpose
Turn an ambiguous customer problem into validated technical requirements, constraints, risks, and success criteria before proposing a solution.

## When to use
Use at the start of evaluations, architecture workshops, migrations, and complex integrations. Do not use discovery as a disguised product pitch.

## Inputs
Business goals, stakeholders, current architecture, workloads, constraints, timelines, security requirements, operational expectations.

## Context to inspect
Existing systems, ownership boundaries, data flows, integration contracts, incidents, scale, compliance, budget, and decision process.

## Core knowledge
Senior discovery separates stated requests from underlying outcomes. Functional requirements alone are insufficient; latency, availability, security, operability, cost, migration, and organizational constraints frequently determine feasibility.

## Procedure
1. Identify stakeholders and decision owners.
2. Define business outcome and measurable success.
3. Map current-state systems and workflows.
4. Capture functional and non-functional requirements.
5. Surface assumptions, dependencies, constraints, and unknowns.
6. Quantify scale and operational characteristics.
7. Rank risks by impact and uncertainty.
8. Confirm findings with stakeholders before solution design.

## Decision points
Deepen discovery where uncertainty can invalidate architecture or economics. Defer low-impact details that do not affect the decision.

## Common failure patterns
Solutioning too early, accepting vague scale claims, ignoring operations, interviewing only sponsors, and treating assumptions as facts.

## Verification
Stakeholders confirm the current state, requirements, constraints, success metrics, and unresolved questions.

## Expected output
A concise discovery record usable for architecture and evaluation planning.

## Stop conditions
Stop when critical owners are unavailable, requirements conflict materially, or required information cannot be validated.
