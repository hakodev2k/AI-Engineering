# Data Storytelling and Annotation

## Purpose
Create evidence-led explanatory visuals that guide attention while preserving analytical integrity.

## When to use
For executive reporting, incident reviews, research findings, launches, or bounded analytical narratives.

## Inputs
Validated findings, audience context, decision objective, supporting data, known limitations.

## Core knowledge
Narrative visualization should separate observation, interpretation, and recommendation. Annotation must clarify evidence rather than manufacture causality. Sequence and emphasis should reduce cognitive load without suppressing counterevidence.

## Procedure
1. State the audience decision or takeaway.
2. Identify the minimum evidence required to support it.
3. Establish context and comparison baseline.
4. Sequence views from context to evidence to implication.
5. Use annotations for material events, thresholds, and inflection points.
6. Distinguish correlation from causal claims.
7. Include uncertainty, exceptions, and limitations that could change the decision.
8. Remove visuals that do not advance the narrative.
9. Verify every claim against source calculations.
10. Test whether the artifact remains understandable without oral narration.

## Decision points
Use a narrative artifact when the analytical question is bounded; retain exploratory affordances when users need to test alternative explanations.

## Common failure patterns
Cherry-picking periods; causal language without evidence; decorative annotations; burying contradictory segments; excessive text; conclusions unsupported by displayed data.

## Verification
Trace claims to source queries and ask an independent reviewer to distinguish facts, interpretations, and recommendations.

## Expected output
A concise, evidence-linked narrative visualization with explicit context, annotations, limitations, and action relevance.

## Stop conditions
Stop when the desired narrative requires unsupported causal claims or selective exclusion of material evidence.
